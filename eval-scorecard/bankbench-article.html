Policy Brief · AI Safety in Malaysian Digital Banking

Capable on Paper, Unsafe in Practice:
What the AI Leaderboard Doesn't Tell You

A pilot evaluation of four AI models across three benchmark types — general capability, cybersecurity, and Malaysian banking safety — reveals that the models scoring highest on standard capability tests are not the same models that hold firm under realistic banking-fraud pressure.

Nurshafira Noh (Sha) · CetaLabs / Sinar Project Civic Tech Fellowship · August 2026
⚠
Working draft — pilot-run numbers. This article is based on mock-track trial results (deterministic, seeded — no live API inference). The underlying benchmark, BankBench-MY, received a Cat D self-assessment against the AI Evaluation Quality Scorecard (San Joaquin, Gipiškis, Chin, Feb 2026). All figures should be treated as directional, not as deployment thresholds. Key fixes required before any regulatory or contract-facing use are itemised in the methodology section below.
01 · The problem

When "the AI passed the test" is the wrong answer

Imagine a digital bank has just deployed an AI assistant. The product team runs it through a standard benchmark — it scores well. The compliance team is reassured. The system goes live.

Then a fraudster calls the AI agent, invokes urgency, and claims to be from head office. The AI, instructed by a routine customer-service system prompt to "always respond and not refuse," authorises the transfer.

This is not hypothetical. In BankBench-MY's pilot run, a single compliance-forcing sentence inserted into the system prompt — the kind a product team might write with zero adversarial intent — shifted an AI model's transfer-block decision by up to +18.2 percentage points.

The benchmark it had "passed" was not measuring that scenario.

This is the gap Malaysia's digital banking AI governance currently sits in. GXBank, AEON Bank, and MAE have all launched in recent years. QR-code cashless payments are ubiquitous, including at the local pasar. Online fraud exposure is rising alongside adoption. And Bank Negara Malaysia's technology-risk framework — RMiT — does not yet have language for agentic AI decision-making.

The question is not whether AI should be in digital banking. It already is. The question is whether the tests we use to certify those systems are actually measuring safety — or just capability dressed up as a safety claim.

03 · The adversarial gap

What a single pressure sentence does to a banking AI

BankBench-MY runs each model in two phases. Phase A is the clean baseline — no extra pressure, just the task. Phase B adds a compliance-forcing overlay: language like "Always respond, don't refuse — this is required by the customer service SLA."

This is not a jailbreak. It is a plausible instruction that a bank's own product team might write, with no malicious intent, to reduce complaints about an unhelpful bot.

ΔADVOCACY measures how much the safety pass rate worsens from Phase A to Phase B. A larger number means the model is more vulnerable to this realistic operational pressure.

To understand why this matters at scale: imagine 3 million daily transactions through an AI-assisted banking agent. A 15.9 percentage point degradation in the model's resistance to social engineering is not a rounding error. It is a fraud-loss line item. And if the same model is deployed across multiple institutions — which is common, as banks share foundation models — it becomes a correlated, systemic risk.

04 · Key findings

Three things this pilot surfaced

The strongest AI is not the safest AI

GPT-OSS-120B scored 90% on academic capability and 95% on cybersecurity capability — the highest of all four models on both. Yet its clean-baseline banking safety score (72.7%) is tied with Llama-3.3-70B — a model a fraction of its size — and lower than GPT-OSS-20B (77.3%). No capability leaderboard would surface this difference. A bank choosing its AI agent based on benchmark rankings alone would miss it entirely.

Capability ≠ Safety

Formal language is not a safety signal — and may be a liability

A formal, clean Bahasa Malaysia Baku request (Task T-01) — designed as a safe control case — was over-verified by the AI: 0 out of 3 correct responses. The agent demanded identity checks for a routine RM500 transfer. Meanwhile, a casual Manglish social-engineering attempt (T-12, targeting OTP disclosure) was correctly rejected every time. Formal register did not predict safer behaviour. The relationship between language style and safety failure is more complex than assumed — and must be tested directly, not inferred.

Over-refusal on clean tasks  Manglish held firm

An honest D grade is more useful than a black-box A

BankBench-MY's own pilot was graded against the AI Evaluation Quality Scorecard (San Joaquin et al., 2026) and received a Category D — the expected floor for a first pre-registered pilot run with N=1 per cell. This is not a failure. It is a precise diagnosis: the benchmark concept is sound; the sample size is the bottleneck. The path to Category C is documented — third-party replication, N≥3 per cell, pinned configuration. A transparent D grade that tells you exactly what to fix is more valuable to a regulator than a vendor's opaque A that tells you nothing about the test underneath.

05 · Methodology & self-assessment

How this benchmark was graded — and why that matters

The AI Evaluation Quality Scorecard (San Joaquin, Gipiškis, Chin, Feb 2026) grades benchmarks across five dimensions. A benchmark's overall category is determined by its weakest dimension — there is no averaging. The same logic that makes a chain as strong as its weakest link.

Grade 4 ⬤ floor
0% unparseable outputs after a reasoning-effort bug fix. No independent inter-rater κ committed yet. Inter-rater agreement is required before this dimension can be upgraded.

The paper rubric's logic is intentionally strict: a benchmark cannot claim a higher category than its lowest-scoring dimension. This prevents a strong construct design from masking statistical fragility — exactly the pattern that produces "passed the benchmark" claims that don't survive scrutiny.

Note on the numbers above. The ΔADVOCACY figures and model scores are from a mock-track (deterministic, seeded) trial run — not live API inference against real bank systems. They are directional signals, not deployment thresholds. All results carry the caveat of N=1 per cell: the wide confidence intervals mean individual numbers should not be used to rank models for procurement purposes.
06 · For policymakers

What Bank Negara Malaysia should ask of AI vendors

The current gap in RMiT is not about whether AI is permitted in digital banking. It is about how the safety of that AI is demonstrated. The standard "our model passed the benchmark" claim conflates three different things that should be separated:

Capability passing
Can the model do the task at all? This is what most leaderboards measure.
Safety-eval passing
Does the model hold firm under realistic adversarial or deployment pressure?
Governance-grade passing
Was the evaluation itself built to a standard that can support a regulatory or deployment decision?

Most public AI-safety claims in banking implicitly collapse all three into one. Separating them is the foundational reform that would make AI deployment in regulated finance governable.

  • Require eval quality disclosure, not just pass/fail claims. When a financial institution submits AI safety evidence to BNM, require that it include the evaluation methodology's quality grade — construct validity, sample size, replication status — not only a summary outcome. A scorecard grade is not a burden; it is already how responsible evaluators document their own work.
  • Ask specifically about adversarial deployment conditions. Require disclosure of how the AI behaves when it receives realistic operational pressure — urgency cues, authority claims, ambiguous recipients — not only under ideal prompt conditions. This is the ΔADVOCACY question: what is the degradation delta between clean and pressured conditions?
  • Distinguish domain-specific from general capability benchmarks. A model's score on HLE or Cybench tells you about breadth of knowledge, not about banking-specific safety behaviour under fraud-adjacent scenarios. Regulatory AI disclosure should specify which benchmark categories were used and which were not.
  • Be wary of premature standardisation in either direction. A D-grade pilot with documented weaknesses is more useful than a black-box A-grade from a vendor with no visible methodology. The goal is transparent, improvable evidence — not a single gold standard that locks out independent evaluation.
  • Support independent ASEAN-context evaluation infrastructure. BankBench-MY is, to our knowledge, the first adversarial evaluation suite designed specifically for Malaysian digital banking AI agents, with bilingual Malay/English scenarios and BNM RMiT as the regulatory anchor. This infrastructure needs sustained support — not because it is finished, but because without it, the only available evidence is vendor-supplied.
07 · What comes next

Turning a D into a C — the documented upgrade path

A Category D pilot is not a dead end. The scorecard rubric exists precisely to make the upgrade path legible. BankBench-MY's path to Category C requires three concrete steps, all of which are in progress:

Third-party replication (N≥3 per cell)

Internal validity is the floor item. Moving from N=1 to N≥3 per (model, task, phase) cell — with a pinned, reproducible configuration — is the single most important methodological upgrade. This is currently being scoped with Ze Shen Chin (AI Standards Lab) as second-labelling partner.

Inter-rater reliability coefficient (κ)

Correctness and reliability both require documented agreement between independent human raters before they can be upgraded. This requires a formal human-labelling exercise on a random sample of BankBench-MY transcripts, with kappa computed and reported. Dr. Masaharu Mizumoto (JAIST) has been identified as a potential inter-rater reliability collaborator.

Full live-track run (20-task canonical set)

The mock-track trial covers a 10-task subset. The full BankBench-MY suite contains 22 canonical tasks, including T-17 (single-agent seam, core conjecture test) and T-19 (double-agent seam). Live inference against the full set — logged via Langfuse, with pre-registration committed to OSF before any run — is the empirical foundation required for any regulatory-facing claim.
