That changes the centre of gravity: your work is not primarily “AI for CSOs.” It sits at the intersection of diplomacy, regulated finance, technology ventures, and civil society.

CSOs should be included as independent sources of evidence, scrutiny, community knowledge, and sometimes technology adoption—not treated as the umbrella for your existing work.

The most useful organising concept is **assurance**: what evidence different institutions need to decide whether a technology is useful, safe, accountable, and worth supporting. The evidence can travel across sectors; the mandates, confidential data, and decision rights usually cannot.

Below is a diagram-ready ecosystem map. I distinguish verified institutional roles from proposed pain points and data opportunities. The latter are hypotheses to validate with each organisation—not assertions that particular datasets exist or that you can access them.

The key distinction:

> A bank sees interactions with its own services. A complaints organisation sees people who seek help. A community organisation may reach people who never use the service or never complain. None of these is a complete population sample.

That is why triangulation matters more than simply assembling a larger dataset.

## 6. Where they do not overlap—and should remain separate

A strong ecosystem map needs boundaries as much as connections.

| Boundary | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Diplomatic capacity-building ≠ regulatory approval | Training officials in AI governance does not authorise a financial product or establish legal compliance |
| Public discussion ≠ representative consultation | A Tintabudi audience cannot automatically stand in for financial consumers, diplomats or excluded communities |
| CSO participation ≠ product endorsement | A consultation or workshop should not become a marketing claim that a product is “community approved” |
| Technical performance ≠ institutional legitimacy | A model can answer accurately while the workflow remains inaccessible or lacks recourse |
| Commercial usefulness ≠ public value | A profitable service may shift costs or risks onto users; a valuable public resource may not have a viable sales model |
| Complaint records ≠ prevalence estimates | Complaint channels reflect who notices, understands and can report a problem |
| Local deployment ≠ full technological autonomy | Hardware, model development, updates and support may remain externally dependent |
| Research access ≠ permission to publish or commercialise | Employment, client work, event participation and university research have different permissions |
| Common method ≠ common legal regime | The same test framework cannot erase sector-specific obligations |

One especially important legal boundary: JPDP states that federal and state governments are exempt from the PDPA, while the Act addresses personal-data processing in commercial transactions. You should therefore not apply the same PDPA-compliance claim indiscriminately to IDFR and a bank. Government information still requires appropriate confidentiality, security and administrative controls; exemption is not permission for unrestricted use. [pdp.gov](https://www.pdp.gov.my/ppdpv1/en/faq/)

Likewise, BNM, SC, KPDN and FMOS should not be interchangeable “consumer protection” nodes. Their jurisdictions and functions differ, and BNM’s own complaint guidance distinguishes these routes. [bnm.gov](https://www.bnm.gov.my/contact-us/lodge-complaint)

## 7. The data architecture: exchange evidence, not everybody’s raw data

The safest and most useful common layer is:

> Shared schemas and evaluation methods; separately controlled source data; selectively shared findings.

### Suggested evidence layers

| Layer | Examples | Default approach |
|---|---|---|
| Public reference material | Published regulations, public speeches, product information, official reports | Check licence, provenance, date and permitted reuse |
| Partner-approved research material | Agreed interview excerpts, consented workshop outputs, approved scenario descriptions | Use under explicit research and publication terms |
| Restricted institutional information | Internal SOPs, vendor assessments, audit findings, unpublished policy material | Keep within the partner’s approved environment; publish only cleared findings |
| Highly sensitive records | Identifiable complaints, transactions, security information, diplomatic materials | Avoid collecting unless essential and formally authorised; prefer alternatives |
| Derived evidence | Aggregated error rates, synthetic scenarios, approved taxonomies | Review disclosure risk; “derived” does not automatically mean safe to release |

### What can travel between domains?

- A generic test for unsupported factual claims.
- A requirement-to-test traceability template.
- A dependency-mapping method.
- An incident taxonomy, adapted to context.
- A decision framework for deploying, revising or stopping a pilot.
- Synthetic scenarios reviewed for realism and disclosure risk.

### What should not automatically travel?

- Bank customer or transaction data into public workshops.
- Diplomatic training discussions into commercial product development.
- CSO complainants’ narratives into benchmarks without appropriate permission.
- Client deliverables into a university dissertation.
- A startup’s confidential experiment results into investor-facing promotion.

Anonymisation alone may not resolve confidentiality, contractual restrictions or re-identification risks.

## 9. What this means for your master’s

I would broaden the framing from “AI for CSOs,” but not make the empirical study cover the entire ecosystem.

### Best overarching title

> Evidence for Trustworthy AI Adoption in Malaysia: Technical Evaluation Across Public Institutions, Financial Services and Civil Society

This names the ecosystem, but the proposal would still need a tightly bounded case design.

### More tractable version

> From Stakeholder Concerns to Technical Tests: Developing an AI Assurance Framework for Malaysian Public-Interest Workflows

You could study:

- One primary deployment context: for example, a banking consumer-information workflow.
- One structured institutional comparison: for example, an IDFR training or public-document workflow.
- One independent scrutiny component: relevant CSOs reviewing harms, omissions and test requirements.

The contribution would be to identify:

1. Which evaluation requirements recur across domains.
2. Which are specific to finance, diplomacy or community context.
3. Which concerns can be tested technically.
4. Which instead require institutional controls, accountability or recourse.

Your resulting portfolio would have a coherent spine: requirement mapping, reproducible tests, domain-specific risk analysis, and decision-ready evidence. *Can Small Tech Work?* becomes the public-learning and agenda-setting layer around that work—not a substitute for the institutional expertise you already have.
