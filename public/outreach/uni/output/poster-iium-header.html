01

Introduction

GenAI produces fluent, confident outputs — whether or not they are grounded. Fabricated citations, invented fatwas, and misattributed hadith are indistinguishable from accurate ones without external verification (Walters & Wilder, 2023), yet students use these tools daily with no framework for judging when to trust.

The Islamic epistemological tradition distinguishes four degrees of assent to a proposition (Al-Attas, 1995). CETALab Uni uses these four states as its framework, training students to recognise, weigh, and verify before accepting.

Four research objectives.

R01 Measure how accurately students classify AI responses by the four epistemic states.
R02 Identify the direction of misclassification — over-trust vs under-trust.
R03 Examine changes in self-reported awareness before and after the module.
R04 Evaluate students' perceptions of relevance and usefulness.

04

Conclusion

Students entered the module with confidence that exceeded their ability to judge AI output. They distinguished epistemic states at 39.1% accuracy — above chance, but only just — and their weakest point was exactly where the risk is greatest: more than half of fabricated responses were judged probable or certain.

The module corrected their self-assessment: self-rated detection ability fell from 3.86 to 3.45 (p = .042) while actual classification held — evidence of recalibration, not decline. Students overwhelmingly rated the module as relevant (86.4%) and reported that it helped them understand how AI actually behaves (81.8%).

For practice. Lecturers should treat AI output on religious questions as material to be verified, not as a source. AI literacy activities should include deliberately fabricated examples.

For the next DBR cycle. Independent expert validation of the expected states, an added comparison group, lecturers included as participants, and repeated use tested for retention of detection skill.
